# Brand Guide — Banana English

## Name decision

**Banana English** — renamed from Papaya on 2026-08-23 at the founder's decision.

Store listing: "Banana English", subtitle "Speak English with confidence".

**The core rationale — this is the thing to build the brand on:**

**กล้วยๆ (gluay-gluay) is Thai slang for "easy peasy".** So to a Thai speaker the name reads as
*"English? Easy."* — which is a promise aimed squarely at this audience's documented barrier: not
ability, but the fear of speaking and losing face. Papaya said *"Thai"*. Banana says *"you can do
this"*, in Thai, without translation.

That is a stronger asset than the som-tam association it replaces, and it is invisible to non-Thai
speakers, which is exactly right for a Thai-only audience: the joke belongs to the user.

⚠️ **The name also reverses two decisions the earlier guide argued for. Both were deliberate, and both
are recorded here so they are not re-litigated by accident:**

1. **"English" is now inside the name.** The earlier rationale was that "English" is generic,
   unprotectable, and belongs in the subtitle so the trademark rests on the distinctive word alone.
   Under the new name only "Banana" is ownable, and "Banana" is a far more crowded mark than "Papaya"
   (Banana Republic being the obvious one). **Trademark clearance is now more important, not less** —
   get an attorney opinion on "Banana English" as a whole mark in classes 9 and 41 before any spend.
2. **The Thai-food connection is gone.** Papaya carried som tam — green papaya salad, arguably the most
   iconic Thai dish — which is where "From som tam to small talk" came from. Bananas are everywhere in
   Thai cooking (kluay tod, kluay buat chi, banana leaf) but carry no single signature dish, so the
   warmth has to come from the mascot and voice rather than the name.

**Names rejected, and why** (kept for context so we don't re-litigate):
- *Pickle* / *Picklo* / *Pickle Up* — rich wordplay (ฝรั่งดอง, "in a pickle" = stuck), but crowded: Picklo has a live Australian commercial user; Pickle Up is occupied by a pickleball app in both app stores.
- *Mango* / *Mango English* — direct collision with **Mango Languages**, an established ESL app with a Thai course. Too close in name and category to be safe.
- *Farang*, *Guava*, *Durian*, etc. — considered as fallback fruit options; Guava remains a clean backup if Banana English ever becomes unavailable.

**⚠️ One risk to check with testers, not a blocker:**
In English, "banana" is a pejorative for Asian people — "yellow on the outside, white on the inside",
someone seen as having lost their culture. The Thai reading (กล้วยๆ = easy) is the one our users will
reach for first, and it dominates. But our users live in Australia, the UK and the US, so they also
live inside the English reading. **Ask two or three testers what the name says to them, unprompted,
before spending on trademark or design.** Expected answer: "easy". If anyone reaches for the slur
instead, that is worth knowing before launch rather than after.

**Why Banana English works:**
- **กล้วยๆ = easy.** The name is a promise in the user's own language, and it targets the fear rather than the skill.
- Open in the language-learning category — no direct competitor found in app stores or visible trademark search.
- Easy for Thai speakers to pronounce: ba-na-na is three open syllables, no clusters, no final consonants, no `/r/`–`/l/`. The name itself models good pronunciation, which is on-brand for a pronunciation app. (This survives the rename — it was true of Papaya for the same reasons.)
- Bananas are everywhere in Thai daily life and cooking (kluay tod, kluay buat chi, banana leaf) — familiar and homey rather than exotic, and instantly legible to everyone else as a friendly fruit.
- **Audience is Thai-only**, which defuses the one real risk (banana as Spanish-language slang matters only if courting Spanish-speaking ESL learners — not our audience) and keeps the som-tam meaning consistent across every target country.

## Outstanding legal work (not yet done — do before public launch)
- Proper trademark clearance for "Banana English" in AU (IP Australia) in classes 9 (software) and 41 (education) before committing further.
- Same clearance for US (USPTO) and UK (UKIPO) — **now business-critical, not deferred**: AU alone caps
  around $130k/yr, so the UK and US diaspora markets are where the business actually is. Budget for this
  earlier than "as each market is entered" implies. EU (EUIPO) as each market is entered — trademarks are territorial; AU clearance does not cover other countries. Consider the Madrid Protocol to extend from one base filing.
- US needs particular care: existing US marks include Banana English Inc. (housewares/stationery, different class), Banana English Health (education class, different subject), and Banana English Global (payroll, offices in AU/UK/US) — none block the category directly but the crowding warrants an attorney opinion before the US launch.
- Domain/handle check: banana.com is certainly gone — evaluate bananaenglish.com, banana.app, or a "getbanana/usebanana" form, kept consistent across app stores and social handles.

## Visual identity
- **Icon concept**: needs redrawing. The papaya concept (cross-section, seed cluster forming a speech bubble) does not port — a banana has no cross-section to draw. Options: a peeled banana curved into a speech-bubble tail, or a bunch where one banana is "speaking". **Unresolved.**
- **Palette** (banana, 2026-08-23):

  | Token | Hex | Use |
  |---|---|---|
  | Banana yellow | `#FFCE1F` | Primary. Buttons, header, active states |
  | Deep peel | `#8A6D00` | Yellow as *text* or a border on white |
  | Coral | `#F4603E` | Recording state, "work on this" |
  | Leaf green | `#5C9E31` | Success, progress bars |
  | Seed charcoal | `#2E2A2B` | All body text, and text **on** yellow |
  | Banana cream | `#FFF9E6` | Page background |
  | Muted | `#7A716B` | Secondary text |

  **Yellow always takes charcoal text, never white.** White on `#FFCE1F` is **1.49:1** — unreadable and
  a WCAG failure. Charcoal on yellow is **9.5:1**. This is the one rule that cannot be broken for
  aesthetics: our users are reading a second language on a phone, often in bad light, and low contrast
  costs them comprehension before it costs them taste. Where yellow must appear as text or a thin
  border on white, use Deep peel `#8A6D00` instead.
- **Mascot**: "Nana" (from ba-NA-na; was "Paya" from pa-PAY-a) — a round, warm, encouraging banana character. Cheers attempts, never scolds; drives streak/nudge notifications. Fear of losing face is a real barrier for Thai learners, so the mascot's job is to make speaking feel safe. The กล้วยๆ meaning gives Nana a natural catchphrase: whatever the learner just managed, it was **easy**.
- **Tagline (store)**: "Speak English with confidence."
- **Tagline (community/Thai marketing)**: **"อังกฤษ? กล้วยๆ"** — *"English? Easy peasy."* Built on the
  name's Thai meaning, and it answers the fear directly. (Replaces "From som tam to small talk", which
  died with the papaya name — som tam is green *papaya* salad.) ⚠️ Thai wording to be confirmed by the
  founder.

## Who the user actually is (2021 Census, Thailand-born in Australia)

Confirmed 2026-08-23 — see `docs/feasibility-assessment.md`. This is not a marketing persona, it's the
census.

- **70.1% female.** Write, illustrate, and market for her by default. A gender-neutral-by-accident brand
  is leaving the actual audience on the table.
- **Median personal income $657/week** (national median $805). Price sensitivity is real; so is the
  need — English is tied to earning more, not to self-improvement.
- **52.4% aged 25–44**, phone-first, frequently working irregular shifts in hospitality, food service,
  and care. Sessions must work in five spare minutes, not a study hour.
- **45.7% are not citizens** — navigating visas, Medicare enrolment, tenancy, and school enrolment right
  now. That urgency is the brand's emotional centre.

The mascot's job — making speaking feel safe despite fear of losing face — is the right instinct and it
matters more for this audience than the palette does.

## Voice and tone
Warm, encouraging, judgment-free — never mimics a strict teacher. Corrections are framed as "try this" not "you're wrong." Thai-language explanations use a peer/older-sibling tone, not a textbook tone.

**Positioning constraint:** the free alternative is AMEP (unlimited free government English classes, ~300
locations, free childcare). Never position Banana English as "learn English" in the abstract — that fight is lost
to a free competitor. Position it as *rehearse the specific conversation you're dreading this week, until
it's automatic, in five minutes, on your phone, at 11pm.* Also avoid leaning on "practise with AI" — that
is becoming a bundled commodity feature in cheaper mass-market apps.
